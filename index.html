Singular Indefinite Subjects

Everybody / Everything / Nobody / Nothing / Somebody / Something / Anybody / Anything

Core Rule

The subjects Everybody, Everything, Nobody, Nothing, Somebody, Something, Anybody, Anything are always singular. Hence, they always take a singular verb and singular pronouns.

Examples

✓
Everybody is ready to do his work.
Everybody is singular, so use "is" (not "are") and "his" (not "their").
✗
Anybody can do the work if they try
→ Replace with "he tries" because anybody is singular.
✓
Nothing but some books was available.
Here "but" acts as a preposition. The main subject before the preposition is "Nothing" which is singular.
✗
Nothing but some students was present
→ Replace "nothing" with "nobody" because some students are persons. Use "nobody" to indicate persons.
✓
Something is missing.
✓
Some things are missing.
Note: "Some things" is plural, unlike "Something" which is singular.
✓
Everybody who wants cake should pass his plate.
✗
Everyone section of the society is feeling the pinch.
→ Replace "Everyone" with "Every" because "section" is a noun and "everyone" is a pronoun. A noun and pronoun cannot come together — change pronoun to adjective "every".

Question Tag Rules

→
Somebody was present, weren't they?
Somebody/Nobody are used for persons — use plural question tag.
→
Something was present, wasn't it?
Something/Nothing are used for things — use singular question tag.
→
Nobody was present, were they?
2

Each / Every / Either / Neither / Any / None

Understanding quantity determiners

Key Distinctions

✓ When to Use
  • Each: 2 or more persons/things (pronoun & adjective)
  • Every: More than 2 only (always adjective)
  • Either: 2 persons/things (positive sentences)
  • Any: More than 2 (positive sentences)
  • Neither: 2 persons/things (negative sentences)
  • None: More than 2 (negative sentences)
✗ Common Mistakes
  • Every of the girls → use "Each" (need pronoun subject)
  • None of the two → use "Neither"
  • Any of the two → use "Either"
  • Neither of three → use "None"

Examples

✓
Each girl was present.
✓
Each of the girls was present.
✓
Every girl was present.
✗
Every of the girls was present.
→ Replace "every" with "each" — we need a subject (noun/pronoun) and "each" can act as pronoun, but "every" is only an adjective.
✓
Each student wishes to complete his work on time.
✓
Each of the students wishes to complete his work.
After "each of", use plural noun (students) but singular verb (wishes).
✗
None of the two students did the work.
→ Replace "None" with "Neither" — "none" is for more than 2, "neither" is for exactly 2.
✗
Mr. Sharma has three sons, neither of them is hardworking.
→ Replace "Neither" with "None" — three sons = more than 2.
✗
Any of the two students will complete the project.
→ Replace "Any" with "Either" — "either" is for 2 persons/things.
✓
None of the pens is working.
✓
Neither of them attended the function, did they?
✓
Please take one of each. (not "every")
✓
You gave me four toys, and none of them works. (negative sentence)
3

Both...And

Used in positive sentences only

Core Rule

"Both" can be used as a pronoun and an adjective. With "Both," the verb is always plural. "Both" always pairs with "and" — never with "as well as."

Examples

✓
Both were present. (pronoun)
✓
Both the students were present. (adjective)
✗
Both the students as well as the teacher attended the meeting.
→ Replace "as well as" with "and" — "both" always pairs with "and".
✓
Neither of them went to the cinema. → No improvement (this is already correct)
✓
Both my brother and my friends love cricket.
4

Proximity Rule (Either-Or / Neither-Nor)

Verb agrees with the nearer subject

Core Rule

When two subjects are joined by Either...or / Neither...nor / Not only...but also, the verb agrees with the subject nearer to the verb.

Examples

✗
Neither the colour or the size of the clothes suits me.
→ Replace "or" with "nor" — "neither" always pairs with "nor". By proximity rule, "the size" is nearer (singular), so verb "suits" is singular.
✓
Not only Ram but also his friends were present.
"friends" is nearer to the verb, so use plural "were".
✓
Either the students or the principal agrees to attend.
"principal" is nearer to the verb, so use singular "agrees".
✗
Neither of the scout leaders know how to trap wild animals.
→ Replace "know" with "knows" — "neither" takes a singular verb. Here "or" is correct because it connects phrase to phrase (conjunction).
5

Numerical Words (Hundred / Thousand / Million / Dozen)

Adjective form stays singular; noun form can pluralize

Core Rule

Words like hundred, thousand, million, dozen stay in singular form when preceded by a numeral (acting as adjective). They can be plural when used as a noun (with "of").

Key insight: When a word comes before a noun, it's an adjective — and adjectives don't have plural forms in English.

Examples

✓
She has donated five thousand rupees.
"Rupees" is noun. Words before nouns are adjectives. Adjectives don't pluralize.
✗
She has donated five thousands rupees.
→ Replace "thousands" with "thousand" — can't pluralize an adjective.
✓
She has donated thousands of rupees.
Here "thousands" is a noun (before "of"). Nouns can be pluralized.
✗
She brought two dozens eggs.
→ Replace "dozens" with "dozen" — "dozen" is acting as adjective here.
✓
She bought two dozen eggs.
✓
She bought dozens of eggs.
"Dozens" is a noun here (before "of").
✓
Dozens of eggs were bought.
✗
Two-thousands people were present.
→ Replace "thousands" with "thousand" — acting as adjective before noun.
✓
Thousands of people were present.
Correct — "thousands" is a noun before "of".
6

Until / Unless / If / Whether / That

Conjunctions for time, condition, doubt, and certainty

Key Distinctions

WordFunctionUsage
UntilConjunction & PrepositionFor time
UnlessAlways ConjunctionFor condition
IfConjunctionCondition (no "will") OR doubt ("will" OK)
WhetherConjunctionDoubt / Uncertainty
ThatConjunctionCertainty / Surety

The If + Not = Unless Rule

Important: If + not = Unless. Since "unless" already contains the negative meaning, never use "not" with "unless".

Examples

✓
I will wait until he comes. (conjunction — connects clause to clause)
✓
I will wait until Monday. (preposition — before a noun)
✓
If he comes, I will go. (condition — no "will" after "if")
✓
I don't know if he will come. (doubt — "will" is OK after "if")
✗
If they will come, I will go.
→ Remove "will" — here "if" expresses condition, not doubt.
✓
Unless he works, he will not succeed. (condition)
✗
Unless one are not punctual in one's work.
→ Replace "unless one are not" with "if one is" — "unless" is already negative, so "not" is redundant.
✓
I don't know if / whether he will come. (both correct)
✓
I don't know whether he will come or not. (use "whether" with "or")
"If" cannot be used with "or not".
✓
I have few doubts that he will come.
"few" = almost no doubt = certainty → use "that".
✓
I have a few doubts whether he will come.
"a few" = some doubt = uncertainty → use "whether".
7

No Double Comparatives & Superlatives

Never use two degrees of comparison together

Examples

✗
He is more wiser than his brother.
→ Remove "more" — two comparative degrees ("more" + "wiser") cannot come together.
✗
Sachin is capable of doing more better work.
→ Remove "more" — "more" and "better" are both comparative.
✗
She is the most smartest girl in the class.
→ Remove "most" — two superlative degrees cannot come together.
✓
She is the most intelligent girl in the class. (correct — "most" + positive degree)

Special Note

More better ❌ but Much better ✔
Don't use "more" with "better," but "much" is acceptable with comparative forms.
✓
She is feeling much better.
8

"Comparatively" & "Relatively" Take Positive Degree

These words already imply comparison

Core Rule

The words "comparatively" and "relatively" already express comparison within themselves. Therefore, they take adjectives of POSITIVE degree (not comparative).

Examples

✓
The weather is comparatively hot. (not "hotter")
✓
The train is relatively slow. (not "slower")
✓
Earlier job opportunities were comparatively good. (not "better")
✓
It does more harm than good. (not "better" — positive degree before "than")
9

"As...As" Construction & Twice/Thrice

Twice/thrice/two times require "as...as" (not comparative)

Core Rule

When sentences contain twice, thrice, two times, etc., use the "as...as" construction. When "as...as" is used for comparison, positive degree comes between them.

Examples

✗
He is twice taller than his brother.
→ Replace "taller than" with "as tall as".
✗
He is two times taller than his brother.
→ Replace "taller than" with "as tall as".
✓
He is two times as tall as his brother.
✗
He is equally as smart as his brother.
→ Remove "equally" — both "equally" and "as...as" express comparison; using both is redundant.
✗
How many eggs and how many money do you need?
→ Replace second "how many" with "how much" — "egg" is countable, "money" is uncountable. Use "many" with countable, "much" with uncountable.
✗
These all mangoes are ripe.
→ Replace "these all" with "all these" — "this/that/these/those" are demonstrative adjectives and come after "all".
10

Comparing Two Qualities of the Same Person

Use "more" (not "-er") when comparing qualities of one subject

Core Rule

When two qualities of the same person/thing are compared, use "more" (not the "-er" suffix) to form the comparative degree.

Examples

✓
She is smarter than her sister. (comparing two different people — correct)
✗
She is smarter than beautiful.
→ Replace "smarter" with "more smart" — comparing two qualities of the same person.
✓
He is braver than his brother. (comparing two people — correct)
✗
He is braver than wise.
→ Replace "braver" with "more brave" — comparing two qualities of the same person.
11

Main Subject vs Qualifier

Verb agrees with the main subject, not the qualifier

Core Rule

Ensure the verb agrees with the main subject (not the qualifier). Use the preposition "of" to identify: the subject before the preposition is the main subject; the subject after the preposition is the qualifier/object.

Trick: In "X of Y", X = main subject, Y = qualifier. The verb matches X.

Examples

✓
The box of Nestle's chocolates is missing. (box = singular)
✓
The boxes of Nestle's chocolates are missing. (boxes = plural)
✓
The decoration of the rooms is beautiful. (decoration = singular)
✗
A flock of birds were flying over the lake.
→ Replace "were" with "was" — "a flock" is singular.
✗
The prices of tomatoes has soared up to Rs. 65-70.
→ Replace "has" with "have" — "prices" is the main subject (plural).
✗
A letter of recommendation from the principal and HOD have helped him.
→ Replace "have" with "has" — main subject is "a letter" (singular).
✓
One-third of the pillar has rotted away. (pillar = singular)
✓
One-third of the pillars have rotted away. (pillars = plural)
✓
Two-thirds of the pillar has rotted away. (pillar = singular)
✗
Two-third of the pillar has rotted away.
→ Replace "two-third" with "two-thirds" — with "two" we use the plural form "thirds".
✗
A third of us feels compelled to check our phones.
→ Replace "feels" with "feel" — main subject is "us" (plural).

Examples

✓
Many a girl goes to college in time. (singular noun + singular verb)
✓
A good many girls go to college in time. (plural noun + plural verb)

13

Relative Pronoun Agreement (Who / That)

Verb agrees with the antecedent before the relative pronoun

Core Rule

When a sentence has a relative pronoun (who/that/which), its subject is the word immediately before the relative pronoun (the antecedent). The verb agrees with that antecedent.

Exception: When "only" precedes "one" — "only one" makes it singular.

Examples

✓
Mr. Sharma is one of our officers who are always alert.
"who" refers to "officers" (plural) → plural verb "are".
✓
Mr. Sharma is only one of our officers that is always alert.
"only one" = singular → singular verb "is".
✓
Mr. Sharma is one of our only officers that are always alert.
"only officers" = plural sense → plural verb "are".
✓
It is I who am responsible for the mistake.
"who" refers to "I" (singular) → "am".
✓
He is one of the best writers that have ever lived.
"that" refers to "writers" (plural) → "have".
14

Conditional Sentences

No two future/modal tenses in one conditional sentence

Three Types of Conditionals

Conditional Sentence Structure
TypeIf ClauseMain ClauseExample
Type 1Simple PresentSimple FutureIf he comes, I will go.
Type 2Simple PastSimple PastIf he came, I would go.
Type 3Past PerfectWould Have + V3If he had come, I would have gone.
Key Rule: Never use "would have" in the if-clause. Similarly, you cannot have two "will" or two "would" in one conditional sentence.

Examples

✗
If he would have read the instructions carefully, he would not have committed mistakes.
→ Replace "would have read" with "had read" — the if-clause must use Past Perfect (not "would have").
✗
If we had Mohan in our team, we would have won the match.
→ Add "had" after "we" → "If we had had Mohan" — the second part is "would have" (past perfect), so the first part must also be past perfect.
15

Time Reference vs Action Reference

Time = Simple Past | Action = Perfect Tense

Core Rule

If a sentence adds a time reference (yesterday, last night, ago), use Simple Past. If it adds an action reference (without specific time), use Present Perfect / Past Perfect.

Examples

✗
He has left for London last night.
→ Replace "has left" with "left" — "last night" is a time reference → Simple Past.
✓
He has left for London. (action reference → Present Perfect)
✗
She had seen the movie yesterday.
→ Replace "had seen" with "saw" — "yesterday" is time reference.
✗
Two years ago, the earthquake had destroyed the city.
→ Replace "had destroyed" with "destroyed" — "two years ago" is time reference.
✗
I was hearing the dog barking last evening.
→ Replace "was hearing" with "heard" — "last evening" is time reference.
✗
Newton has discovered that the force of gravitation makes apples fall.
→ Replace "has discovered" with "discovered" — general fact, not a recent action.
✓
I have taken my lunch. (action reference → Present Perfect)
✗
I have taken my lunch an hour ago.
→ Replace "have taken" with "took" — "an hour ago" is time reference.
16

"It's Time" / "It's High Time" / "It's About Time"

Always followed by Simple Past tense

Examples

✓
It's time he went to school. (not "goes" or "had gone")
✓
It's time to do the work. (correct — infinitive after "time" is also OK)
✓
It's high time he did the work. (not "does")
17

Suppositional Sentences (I Wish / As If / As Though)

Unfulfilled wishes take Simple Past with "were"

Core Rule

Sentences with "I wish," "He/She wishes," "as if," "as though" indicating an unfulfilled wish or desire take Simple Past tense. Always use "were" (plural form) for all subjects, including singular ones.

Examples

✓
I wish I were a doctor. (not "was")
✓
If she were a bird, she would fly.
✓
He behaves as if he knew everything. (not "knows")
✓
He commands as though he were my boss. (not "was")
18

Nouns Joined by Prepositions

Both nouns singular, verb singular — nouns must be the same word

Examples

✗
Women after women spoke against the cruel practices of dowry.
→ Replace with "Woman after woman" — both nouns should be singular.
✗
Days by days, he is becoming weaker.
→ Replace with "Day by day".
✓
Pile on pile of books was arranged.
✓
Page after page of the Gita was read.
✗
He enquired from doors to doors.
→ Replace with "door to door".
19

Apostrophe with "Else"

's goes on "else" when preceded by somebody/anyone/etc.

Core Rule

When pronouns like somebody, anybody, everyone, something are followed by "else", the apostrophe 's is placed on "else" (not on the pronoun).

Note: Two apostrophes cannot come together in a sentence. If two possessives are needed, restructure: "Ram's uncle's friend" → "Friend of Ram's uncle."

Examples

✓
Somebody else's purse has been stolen.
✗
Somebody's else purse has been stolen.
→ Apostrophe goes on "else" → "Somebody else's".
20

Separate vs Joint Possession

Separate = separate apostrophes | Joint = apostrophe on last noun only

Core Rule

When two nouns joined by "and" have separate possession, each gets its own apostrophe. When they have joint possession, only the last noun gets the apostrophe.

Examples

✗
I went to Ram's and Sita's house and found the couple missing.
→ Replace "Ram's" with "Ram" — they live together (joint possession), so apostrophe only on last noun → "Ram and Sita's house".
✓
Mala's and Priya's account. (separate accounts)
✗
Mala's and Priya's joint account.
→ Replace "Mala's" with "Mala" — it's a joint account → "Mala and Priya's joint account".
21

"One and a Half" Agreement

Plural noun + plural verb | "A ... and a half" = singular

Examples

✗
One and a half hour.
→ Use "hours" (plural) — "one and a half" takes plural noun.
✓
One and a half hours.
✓
An hour and a half. (singular noun + singular)
✓
One and a half years were wasted. (plural verb)
✓
A year and a half was wasted. (singular verb)

"Cousin" & Redundant Words

Don't add brother/sister after cousin when gender is clear

Cousin Rule

✗
He is my cousin brother.
→ Remove "brother" — "He" already indicates male gender.
✗
She is my cousin sister.
→ Remove "sister" — "She" already indicates female gender.
✓
I went to the temple with my cousin sister. (gender not clear from context)

Removing Redundant Words

Two nouns with the same meaning cannot come together. Remove the redundant one.

✗
It was a blunder mistake.
→ Remove "mistake" — "blunder" and "mistake" are both nouns with the same meaning.
✗
The angry mob attacked the police officers.
→ Remove "angry" — an angry crowd is already called a "mob".
✗
He is a coward person.
→ Remove "person" — both are nouns with the same meaning.

24

Modal Verbs + V1 (First Form)

Should / Can / Could / May / Might / Will / Shall / Must → always take V1

Core Rule

All modal verbs (should, can, could, may, might, will, shall, must, ought to) are always followed by the verb's 1st form (V1) — no "to," no "ing," no past tense.

Examples

✗
You should not boasting of your achievements.
→ Replace "boasting" with "boast" — modal + V1.
✗
The pedestrians should to be cautious.
→ Replace "to be" with "be" — modal + V1 (remove "to").
✗
The conference will been a celebration.
→ Replace "been" with "be" — modal + V1.
✗
We should honest in our dealings.
→ Add "be" after "should" → "should be honest" — when modal is followed by an adjective, add "be".
✗
She must not to waste time.
→ Replace "to waste" with "waste" — modal + V1.
✗
A new chapter may been put forth.
→ Replace "been" with "be" — modal + V1.
25

Should — Advice vs Condition

Past of "shall" = should (advice) or would (no advice)

Core Rules

  • Should can express both advice and condition
  • Past of "shall" = "should" (when giving advice) or "would" (when no advice)
  • Had better = duty/advice, takes V1, present tense meaning

Examples

✓
Should he work, he will succeed. (condition — inverted form)
This means the same as "If he works, he will succeed."
✓
He said, "I shall do the work." → He said he would do the work.
No advice → use "would".
✓
He said, "We should respect our elders." → They should respect their elders.
Advice → keep "should".
✓
You had better go to school on time. (V1, not "to go" or "gone")
✗
You had better to stop taking medicine.
→ Replace "to stop" with "stop" — "had better" takes V1.
26

"Used To" — Modal Verb vs Adjective

Modal → V1 | Adjective → Gerund (ing) or Noun

Core Rule

"Used to" can function as a modal verb (takes V1) or an adjective (takes gerund -ing form or noun). If there's already a main helping verb (is, was, got), then "used to" is an adjective.

Examples

✓
He used to work hard. (modal verb → V1 "work")
✓
He is used to working hard. (adjective → gerund "working")
"is" is already the main verb, so "used to" is an adjective → takes gerund.
✓
He is used to hard-work. (adjective → noun)
✓
He got used to working hard. (adjective → gerund, "got" is main verb)
✓
Camels are used to carry loads. (passive voice → "used to" is main verb → to + V1)
27

Redundant Modal Combinations

Can/Could + able | May/Might + possible = redundant

Core Rule

Can/Could already means ability — don't add "able/capable". May/Might already means possibility — don't add "possible/likely".

Examples

✗
He may possibly attend the party.
→ Remove "possibly" — "may" already expresses possibility.
✗
It may likely rain.
→ Remove "likely" — "may" already expresses possibility.
✗
I can be able to do the work.
→ Remove "be able to" — "can" already means ability.
✗
The students could not be able to attend the meeting.
→ Remove "be able to".
28

"With a View To" & "Looking Forward To"

Both always take Gerunds (ing form)

Core Rule

Both "with a view to" (= with the purpose of) and "looking forward to" (= expect/anticipate something positive) are prepositional phrases that always take a gerund (ing form) — never a bare verb.

Examples

✓
I come here with a view to teaching. (not "teach")
✓
I am looking forward to meeting you. (not "meet")
✗
Looking forward to hear news about the missing fisherman.
→ Replace "hear" with "hearing" — gerund after "looking forward to".
✗
With a view to avoid traffic accidents, the road was blocked.
→ Replace "avoid" with "avoiding" — gerund after "with a view to".
29

Gerund vs Participle

Gerund = ...ing as Noun | Participle = Verb as Adjective

Key Differences

Gerund (ing form as Noun)
  • Acts as subject or object
  • Takes possessive case before it (his, my, your)
  • Swimming is good for health.
  • I don't mind his staying.
Participle (Verb as Adjective)
  • Present Participle: ...ing as adjective
  • Past Participle: V3 as adjective
  • Takes objective case before it (him, me, us)
  • I saw him leaving.

Examples

✓
Swimming is good for health. (gerund = subject)
✓
He enjoys swimming. (gerund = object)
✓
He is fond of swimming. (gerund = object of preposition)
✓
No smoking. (gerund)
✓
The book is interesting. (present participle = adjective)
✓
The broken glasses were repaired. (past participle = adjective)
✓
I don't mind his staying late hours. (gerund → possessive case)
✗
Due to me being a new comer does not excuse your fault.
→ Replace "me" with "my" — "due to" is a prepositional phrase → gerund follows → possessive case before gerund.
✓
I saw him leaving the house. (participle → objective case)
✓
He saw us parking the car. (participle → objective case)
30

Infinitive & Bare Infinitive

Infinitive = to + V1 | Bare Infinitive = V1 without "to"

Bare Infinitive Triggers

Certain verbs/phrases take Bare Infinitive (V1 without "to"):

Make / Made
→ V1

Separate Auxiliary Verbs for Different Subjects

Different number subjects need separate auxiliaries

Core Rule

When two subjects are different (one singular, one plural), each needs its own auxiliary verb. When both subjects are the same number, they can share one auxiliary.

Examples

✗
Two were killed and one injured.
→ Add "was" after "one" — different number subjects need separate auxiliaries.
✗
One was killed and two injured.
→ Add "were" after "two".
✗
He has and will help the poor.
→ Add "helped" after "has" → "He has helped and will help the poor."
✗
His assistants have and are still doing great jobs.
→ Add "done" after "have" → "have done and are still doing."

Stative Verbs vs Action Verbs

Stative → Adjective | Action → Adverb

Stative Verbs (take ADJECTIVES)

Stative verbs describe a state or situation, not an action. They take adjectives (not adverbs) after them.

Opinion
believe, know, think, support, disagree
Emotion
like, love, hate, need, want, wish, desire, fear
Senses
look, see, appear, taste, sound, hear, feel, seem, smell
Possession
have, own, possess, belong, control
Condition
weigh, lack, involve, resemble, include

Action Verbs (take ADVERBS)

Action verbs express a physical or mental action. They take adverbs after them.

Physical
run, walk, jump, eat, drink, cry, skip, pull, push
Mental
talk, sing, speak, study, teach, try, bring, make, bake

Examples

✓
The birds sang sweetly. (action verb → adverb)
✓
The food tasted sweet. (stative verb → adjective)
✓
She looks beautiful. (stative → adjective)
✓
She ate all the food hungrily. (action → adverb)
✗
He is being very politely.
→ Replace "politely" with "polite" — "being" is stative → adjective.
✗
Mangoes taste more sweetly than any other fruit.
→ Replace "more sweetly" with "sweeter" — "taste" is stative → adjective.
✓
The flowers smell so sweet. (stative → adjective)
✗
The old man is blindly.
→ Replace "blindly" with "blind" — "is" is stative → adjective.

Special Stative Verb Rules

Important: Stative verbs do NOT take "-ing" form in their normal meaning. "Have" (possession), "see" (perception), "believe," "think," "remember," "belong" — none of these form "-ing" in their stative sense.
✓
He is having a meeting tomorrow. ✔ (having = arranged meeting, not possession)
✓
I am having dinner. ✔ (having = eating, not possession)
✗
He is seeing the movie.
→ Replace "seeing" with "watching" — "see" (perception) doesn't take "-ing".
✓
He is seeing the PM tomorrow. ✔ (seeing = meeting, not perception)
✓
Seeing is believing. ✔ (both are gerunds here)
✗
Every year our association is having a meeting.
→ Replace "is having" with "has" — habitual action → Simple Present.
✗
I think our furniture needing to be polished.
→ Replace "needing" with "needs" — "need" is stative, no "-ing". "Furniture" is singular → "needs".
✗
I can't go out as I am not having an umbrella.
→ Replace with "don't have" — "have" (possession) doesn't take "-ing".
✗
I am thinking you were aware of the rules.
→ Replace "am thinking" with "think" — general statement → Simple Present.
✗
Some people are believing that women cannot compete.
→ Replace "are believing" with "believe" — general statement → Simple Present.
✗
Ramesh smiled when he was remembering his hard early years.
→ Replace "was remembering" with "remembered" — "remember" is stative, no "-ing".
✗
People who are not belonging to any party want to divide the country.
→ Replace "are not belonging" with "do not belong" — "belong" is stative, no "-ing".

Personal Pronoun Rules

Case rules for pronouns joined by and/or, after let, it is, than

Rule 1: Same Case with "And" / "Or"

Pronouns joined by "and" or "or" should be in the same case (both subjective or both objective).

✗
These books are for you and I.
→ Replace "I" with "me" — objective case needed after "for".
✓
You and I will do the work. (both subjective)
✗
Ram or myself will do the work.
→ Replace "myself" with "I" — reflexive pronoun cannot be a subject.
✗
There is an understanding between Ram and I.
→ Replace "I" with "me" — objective case after "between".

Rule 2: After "Let" → Objective Case

✓
Let him do the work. (objective case)
✓
Let us attend the meeting.
✗
The principal ordered to let Ram and I enter.
→ Replace "I" with "me" — after "let" use objective case.

Rule 3: After "It is" / "It was" → Subjective Case

✓
It was he who helped me. (not "him")
✓
It is I who am responsible. (not "me")

Rule 4: After "Than" → Depends on Meaning

✓
I know better than he. (subject comparison: I vs he)
✓
I know you better than him. (object comparison: you vs him)
✓
I know you better than he. (subject comparison: he knows you)
Both "him" and "he" are correct after "than" — the meaning changes.

Core Rule

✓

✓

✓

✓

Never, hardly, scarcely, few, little, too, so → subject-verb inversion

Core Rule

✗

✓

✗
Hardly he did attend the class.
→ "Hardly did he attend the class."

Since vs For

Since = point of time | For = duration (length of time)

Key Distinctions

Since (Point of Time)
  • Used with perfect and perfect continuous tenses
  • since January, since Monday, since 2014
  • since childhood, since infancy (stages of life)
  • since ancient/medieval/modern times
  • since time immemorial

  • Used with all three perfect tenses
  • for a month, for a year, for two days

Between = 2 (or distinct items) | Among = more than 2 (as a group)

Core Rule

Between is for 2 things (or more if they are separate/distinct). Among is for more than 2 (as a group). "Between" is never followed by "and" with "among."

Examples

✓
Between the two cats. (2 things)
✓
Among the students. (more than 2, as a group)
✓
Between England, America and Russia. (distinct nations — "between" is OK)
✓
Choose between the singers. (not "among")
✓
Difference between the brothers. (not "among")
✓
Divide the property between the members. (not "among")

Of = possession/cause/material | Off = separation/detachment

Uses of "Of"

  • Possession/ownership: The dramas of Shakespeare
  • Cause/reason: He died of cancer
  • Origin: The water of the Ganga
  • Material: A necklace made of gold
  • Choice/selection: One of the students
