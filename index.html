Singular Indefinite Subjects

Everybody / Everything / Nobody / Nothing / Somebody / Something / Anybody / Anything
⭐☆☆☆☆ (0 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: SSC loves testing whether you recognize words like "everybody," "someone," "nothing" as singular. Watch for:

  • Verb agreement: Everybody is (not "are")
  • Pronoun agreement: Everyone should bring his (not "their")
  • With "but/except": verb agrees with the subject before the preposition
  • Nothing = things, Nobody = persons — don't mix them!

Core Rule

The subjects Everybody, Everything, Nobody, Nothing, Somebody, Something, Anybody, Anything are always singular. Hence, they always take a singular verb and singular pronouns.

Examples

✓
Everybody is ready to do his work.
Everybody is singular, so use "is" (not "are") and "his" (not "their").
✗
Anybody can do the work if they try
→ Replace with "he tries" because anybody is singular.
✓
Nothing but some books was available.
Here "but" acts as a preposition. The main subject before the preposition is "Nothing" which is singular.
✗
Nothing but some students was present
→ Replace "nothing" with "nobody" because some students are persons. Use "nobody" to indicate persons.
✓
Something is missing.
✓
Some things are missing.
Note: "Some things" is plural, unlike "Something" which is singular.
✓
Everybody who wants cake should pass his plate.
✗
Everyone section of the society is feeling the pinch.
→ Replace "Everyone" with "Every" because "section" is a noun and "everyone" is a pronoun. A noun and pronoun cannot come together — change pronoun to adjective "every".

Question Tag Rules

→
Somebody was present, weren't they?
Somebody/Nobody are used for persons — use plural question tag.
→
Something was present, wasn't it?
Something/Nothing are used for things — use singular question tag.
→
Nobody was present, were they?

Each / Every / Either / Neither / Any / None

Understanding quantity determiners
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (15 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Each," "every," "either," "neither" are always singular. Common traps:

  • Each of the boys is (not "are")
  • Neither of the two is (not "are")
  • "None" can be singular OR plural depending on context
  • "A number of" = plural | "The number of" = singular

Key Distinctions

✓ When to Use
  • Each: 2 or more persons/things (pronoun & adjective)
  • Every: More than 2 only (always adjective)
  • Either: 2 persons/things (positive sentences)
  • Any: More than 2 (positive sentences)
  • Neither: 2 persons/things (negative sentences)
  • None: More than 2 (negative sentences)
✗ Common Mistakes
  • Every of the girls → use "Each" (need pronoun subject)
  • None of the two → use "Neither"
  • Any of the two → use "Either"
  • Neither of three → use "None"

Examples

✓
Each girl was present.
✓
Each of the girls was present.
✓
Every girl was present.
✗
Every of the girls was present.
→ Replace "every" with "each" — we need a subject (noun/pronoun) and "each" can act as pronoun, but "every" is only an adjective.
✓
Each student wishes to complete his work on time.
✓
Each of the students wishes to complete his work.
After "each of", use plural noun (students) but singular verb (wishes).
✗
None of the two students did the work.
→ Replace "None" with "Neither" — "none" is for more than 2, "neither" is for exactly 2.
✗
Mr. Sharma has three sons, neither of them is hardworking.
→ Replace "Neither" with "None" — three sons = more than 2.
✗
Any of the two students will complete the project.
→ Replace "Any" with "Either" — "either" is for 2 persons/things.
✓
None of the pens is working.
✓
Neither of them attended the function, did they?
✓
Please take one of each. (not "every")
✓
You gave me four toys, and none of them works. (negative sentence)

Both...And

Used in positive sentences only
⭐☆☆☆☆ (1 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Both...and" always takes a plural verb:

  • Both Ravi and Sita are coming. ✓
  • Compare with "Either...or" → proximity rule applies

Core Rule

"Both" can be used as a pronoun and an adjective. With "Both," the verb is always plural. "Both" always pairs with "and" — never with "as well as."

Examples

✓
Both were present. (pronoun)
✓
Both the students were present. (adjective)
✗
Both the students as well as the teacher attended the meeting.
→ Replace "as well as" with "and" — "both" always pairs with "and".
✓
Neither of them went to the cinema. → No improvement (this is already correct)
✓
Both my brother and my friends love cricket.

Proximity Rule (Either-Or / Neither-Nor)

Verb agrees with the nearer subject
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (12 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: With "either...or" / "neither...nor," the verb agrees with the nearer subject:

  • Neither the teacher nor the students were (students = plural, nearer)
  • Neither the students nor the teacher was (teacher = singular, nearer)

Core Rule

When two subjects are joined by Either...or / Neither...nor / Not only...but also, the verb agrees with the subject nearer to the verb.

Examples

✗
Neither the colour or the size of the clothes suits me.
→ Replace "or" with "nor" — "neither" always pairs with "nor". By proximity rule, "the size" is nearer (singular), so verb "suits" is singular.
✓
Not only Ram but also his friends were present.
"friends" is nearer to the verb, so use plural "were".
✓
Either the students or the principal agrees to attend.
"principal" is nearer to the verb, so use singular "agrees".
✗
Neither of the scout leaders know how to trap wild animals.
→ Replace "know" with "knows" — "neither" takes a singular verb. Here "or" is correct because it connects phrase to phrase (conjunction).

Numerical Words (Hundred / Thousand / Million / Dozen)

Adjective form stays singular; noun form can pluralize
⭐☆☆☆☆ (2 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Hundred," "thousand," "million" stay singular when used as adjectives:

  • Five hundred rupees (not "hundreds")
  • Two thousand people (not "thousands")
  • But: Hundreds of people (noun form = plural)

Core Rule

Words like hundred, thousand, million, dozen stay in singular form when preceded by a numeral (acting as adjective). They can be plural when used as a noun (with "of").

Key insight: When a word comes before a noun, it's an adjective — and adjectives don't have plural forms in English.

Examples

✓
She has donated five thousand rupees.
"Rupees" is noun. Words before nouns are adjectives. Adjectives don't pluralize.
✗
She has donated five thousands rupees.
→ Replace "thousands" with "thousand" — can't pluralize an adjective.
✓
She has donated thousands of rupees.
Here "thousands" is a noun (before "of"). Nouns can be pluralized.
✗
She brought two dozens eggs.
→ Replace "dozens" with "dozen" — "dozen" is acting as adjective here.
✓
She bought two dozen eggs.
✓
She bought dozens of eggs.
"Dozens" is a noun here (before "of").
✓
Dozens of eggs were bought.
✗
Two-thousands people were present.
→ Replace "thousands" with "thousand" — acting as adjective before noun.
✓
Thousands of people were present.
Correct — "thousands" is a noun before "of".

Until / Unless / If / Whether / That

Conjunctions for time, condition, doubt, and certainty
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (5 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: After "unless/until/if/whether," use Simple Present (not "will"):

  • Unless he works hard (not "will work")
  • If it rains, we will stay home (not "will rain")
  • Two future tenses NEVER in one conditional sentence

Key Distinctions

WordFunctionUsage
UntilConjunction & PrepositionFor time
UnlessAlways ConjunctionFor condition
IfConjunctionCondition (no "will") OR doubt ("will" OK)
WhetherConjunctionDoubt / Uncertainty
ThatConjunctionCertainty / Surety

The If + Not = Unless Rule

Important: If + not = Unless. Since "unless" already contains the negative meaning, never use "not" with "unless".

Examples

✓
I will wait until he comes. (conjunction — connects clause to clause)
✓
I will wait until Monday. (preposition — before a noun)
✓
If he comes, I will go. (condition — no "will" after "if")
✓
I don't know if he will come. (doubt — "will" is OK after "if")
✗
If they will come, I will go.
→ Remove "will" — here "if" expresses condition, not doubt.
✓
Unless he works, he will not succeed. (condition)
✗
Unless one are not punctual in one's work.
→ Replace "unless one are not" with "if one is" — "unless" is already negative, so "not" is redundant.
✓
I don't know if / whether he will come. (both correct)
✓
I don't know whether he will come or not. (use "whether" with "or")
"If" cannot be used with "or not".
✓
I have few doubts that he will come.
"few" = almost no doubt = certainty → use "that".
✓
I have a few doubts whether he will come.
"a few" = some doubt = uncertainty → use "whether".

No Double Comparatives & Superlatives

Never use two degrees of comparison together
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (11 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Never combine two comparative/superlative forms — this is a very common SSC trap:

  • more better ✗ → better ✓
  • most best ✗ → best ✓
  • more easier ✗ → easier ✓
  • most easiest ✗ → easiest ✓

Examples

✗
He is more wiser than his brother.
→ Remove "more" — two comparative degrees ("more" + "wiser") cannot come together.
✗
Sachin is capable of doing more better work.
→ Remove "more" — "more" and "better" are both comparative.
✗
She is the most smartest girl in the class.
→ Remove "most" — two superlative degrees cannot come together.
✓
She is the most intelligent girl in the class. (correct — "most" + positive degree)

Special Note

More better ❌ but Much better ✔
Don't use "more" with "better," but "much" is acceptable with comparative forms.
✓
She is feeling much better.

"Comparatively" & "Relatively" Take Positive Degree

These words already imply comparison
⭐☆☆☆☆ (3 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Words like "comparatively," "relatively," "fairly," "rather" already imply comparison — use positive degree after them:

  • Comparatively good (not "better")
  • Relatively easy (not "easier")

Core Rule

The words "comparatively" and "relatively" already express comparison within themselves. Therefore, they take adjectives of POSITIVE degree (not comparative).

Examples

✓
The weather is comparatively hot. (not "hotter")
✓
The train is relatively slow. (not "slower")
✓
Earlier job opportunities were comparatively good. (not "better")
✓
It does more harm than good. (not "better" — positive degree before "than")

"As...As" Construction & Twice/Thrice

Twice/thrice/two times require "as...as" (not comparative)
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (21 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Twice," "thrice," "two times" require "as...as" (not comparative):

  • He is twice as tall as me ✓
  • He is twice taller than me ✗
  • Structure: twice/thrice + as + adj + as

Core Rule

When sentences contain twice, thrice, two times, etc., use the "as...as" construction. When "as...as" is used for comparison, positive degree comes between them.

Examples

✗
He is twice taller than his brother.
→ Replace "taller than" with "as tall as".
✗
He is two times taller than his brother.
→ Replace "taller than" with "as tall as".
✓
He is two times as tall as his brother.
✗
He is equally as smart as his brother.
→ Remove "equally" — both "equally" and "as...as" express comparison; using both is redundant.
✗
How many eggs and how many money do you need?
→ Replace second "how many" with "how much" — "egg" is countable, "money" is uncountable. Use "many" with countable, "much" with uncountable.
✗
These all mangoes are ripe.
→ Replace "these all" with "all these" — "this/that/these/those" are demonstrative adjectives and come after "all".

Comparing Two Qualities of the Same Person

Use "more" (not "-er") when comparing qualities of one subject
⭐☆☆☆☆ (3 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: When comparing two qualities of the same person/thing, use "more" (not "-er"):

  • She is more beautiful than intelligent ✓
  • She is beautifuller than intelligent ✗

Core Rule

When two qualities of the same person/thing are compared, use "more" (not the "-er" suffix) to form the comparative degree.

Examples

✓
She is smarter than her sister. (comparing two different people — correct)
✗
She is smarter than beautiful.
→ Replace "smarter" with "more smart" — comparing two qualities of the same person.
✓
He is braver than his brother. (comparing two people — correct)
✗
He is braver than wise.
→ Replace "braver" with "more brave" — comparing two qualities of the same person.

Main Subject vs Qualifier

Verb agrees with the main subject, not the qualifier
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (17 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Phrases like "as well as," "together with," "along with" do NOT make the subject plural:

  • Ravi, as well as his friends, is coming ✓
  • The captain, along with players, was there ✓
  • Only "and" truly joins subjects into plural

Core Rule

Ensure the verb agrees with the main subject (not the qualifier). Use the preposition "of" to identify: the subject before the preposition is the main subject; the subject after the preposition is the qualifier/object.

Trick: In "X of Y", X = main subject, Y = qualifier. The verb matches X.

Examples

✓
The box of Nestle's chocolates is missing. (box = singular)
✓
The boxes of Nestle's chocolates are missing. (boxes = plural)
✓
The decoration of the rooms is beautiful. (decoration = singular)
✗
A flock of birds were flying over the lake.
→ Replace "were" with "was" — "a flock" is singular.
✗
The prices of tomatoes has soared up to Rs. 65-70.
→ Replace "has" with "have" — "prices" is the main subject (plural).
✗
A letter of recommendation from the principal and HOD have helped him.
→ Replace "have" with "has" — main subject is "a letter" (singular).
✓
One-third of the pillarhas rotted away. (pillar = singular)
✓
One-third of the pillarshave rotted away. (pillars = plural)
✓
Two-thirds of the pillarhas rotted away. (pillar = singular)
✗
Two-third of the pillar has rotted away.
→ Replace "two-third" with "two-thirds" — with "two" we use the plural form "thirds".
✗
A third of us feels compelled to check our phones.
→ Replace "feels" with "feel" — main subject is "us" (plural).

Subject-Verb agreement with special quantifiers
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (10 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Watch the critical difference:

  • Many a student is → singular verb! (despite "many")
  • A number of students are → plural ("a number of" = several)
  • The number of students is → singular ("the number" = the count)

Many a man have come to India from Canada.

Relative Pronoun Agreement (Who / That)

Verb agrees with the antecedent before the relative pronoun
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (5 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: The verb after "who/that/which" agrees with the antecedent (the word before):

  • He is one of those students who work hard (students = plural)
  • He is the only one of those students who works hard (one = singular)
  • The phrase "of those students" is just a qualifier — don't let it confuse you!

Core Rule

When a sentence has a relative pronoun (who/that/which), its subject is the word immediately before the relative pronoun (the antecedent). The verb agrees with that antecedent.

Exception: When "only" precedes "one" — "only one" makes it singular.

Examples

✓
Mr. Sharma is one of our officers who are always alert.
"who" refers to "officers" (plural) → plural verb "are".
✓
Mr. Sharma is only one of our officers that is always alert.
"only one" = singular → singular verb "is".
✓
Mr. Sharma is one of our only officers that are always alert.
"only officers" = plural sense → plural verb "are".
✓
It is I who am responsible for the mistake.
"who" refers to "I" (singular) → "am".
✓
He is one of the best writers that have ever lived.
"that" refers to "writers" (plural) → "have".

Conditional Sentences

No two future/modal tenses in one conditional sentence
⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (50 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: In conditional sentences, never use "will" in the if-clause:

  • If he comes, I will go ✓ (not "will come")
  • If I were rich, I would travel (unreal — use "were" for all persons)
  • Had I known, I would have come (past unreal — inverted form)
  • Zero conditional: If + Present, Present (general truths)

Three Types of Conditionals

Conditional Sentence Structure
TypeIf ClauseMain ClauseExample
Type 1Simple PresentSimple FutureIf he comes, I will go.
Type 2Simple PastSimple PastIf he came, I would go.
Type 3Past PerfectWould Have + V3If he had come, I would have gone.
Key Rule: Never use "would have" in the if-clause. Similarly, you cannot have two "will" or two "would" in one conditional sentence.

Examples

✗
If he would have read the instructions carefully, he would not have committed mistakes.
→ Replace "would have read" with "had read" — the if-clause must use Past Perfect (not "would have").
✗
If we had Mohan in our team, we would have won the match.
→ Add "had" after "we" → "If we had had Mohan" — the second part is "would have" (past perfect), so the first part must also be past perfect.

Time Reference vs Action Reference

Time = Simple Past | Action = Perfect Tense
⭐⭐⭐⭐☆ (31 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Distinguish between time reference and action reference:

  • Time = Simple Past: Before 2020, he lived in Delhi
  • Action = Past Perfect: Before he had finished, the bell rang
  • "After" + action → Past Perfect in the clause before "after"

Core Rule

If a sentence adds a time reference (yesterday, last night, ago), use Simple Past. If it adds an action reference (without specific time), use Present Perfect / Past Perfect.

Examples

✗
He has left for London last night.
→ Replace "has left" with "left" — "last night" is a time reference → Simple Past.
✓
He has left for London. (action reference → Present Perfect)
✗
She had seen the movie yesterday.
→ Replace "had seen" with "saw" — "yesterday" is time reference.
✗
Two years ago, the earthquake had destroyed the city.
→ Replace "had destroyed" with "destroyed" — "two years ago" is time reference.
✗
I was hearing the dog barking last evening.
→ Replace "was hearing" with "heard" — "last evening" is time reference.
✗
Newton has discovered that the force of gravitation makes apples fall.
→ Replace "has discovered" with "discovered" — general fact, not a recent action.
✓
I have taken my lunch. (action reference → Present Perfect)
✗
I have taken my lunch an hour ago.
→ Replace "have taken" with "took" — "an hour ago" is time reference.

Suppositional Sentences (I Wish / As If / As Though)

Unfulfilled wishes take Simple Past with "were"
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (12 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: After "wish," "as if," "as though," "if only" — use Simple Past for present unreal:

  • I wish I were taller (not "was" in formal English)
  • He talks as if he knew everything (not "knows")
  • Past unreal: I wish I had studied (Past Perfect)

Core Rule

Sentences with "I wish," "He/She wishes," "as if," "as though" indicating an unfulfilled wish or desire take Simple Past tense. Always use "were" (plural form) for all subjects, including singular ones.

Examples

✓
I wish I were a doctor. (not "was")
✓
If she were a bird, she would fly.
✓
He behaves as if he knew everything. (not "knows")
✓
He commands as though he were my boss. (not "was")

Nouns Joined by Prepositions

Both nouns singular, verb singular — nouns must be the same word
⭐☆☆☆☆ (0 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: When two singular nouns are joined by prepositions, the verb is singular:

  • The quality of the products is good (not "are")
  • Each boy and each girl is present

Examples

Personal Pronoun Rules

⭐⭐☆☆☆ (9 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: After "let," "it is," "than" — use the correct pronoun case:

  • Let him go (not "he")
  • It is I (formal) / It is me (informal)
  • He is taller than I (formal — "than I am")

Rule 1: Same Case with "And" / "Or"

Pronouns joined by "and" or "or" should be in the same case (both subjective or both objective).

✗
These books are for you and I.
→ Replace "I" with "me" — objective case needed after "for".
✓
You and I will do the work. (both subjective)
✗
Ram or myself will do the work.
→ Replace "myself" with "I" — reflexive pronoun cannot be a subject.
✗
There is an understanding between Ram and I.
→ Replace "I" with "me" — objective case after "between".

Rule 2: After "Let" → Objective Case

✓
Let him do the work. (objective case)
✓
Let us attend the meeting.
✗
The principal ordered to let Ram and I enter.
→ Replace "I" with "me" — after "let" use objective case.

Rule 3: After "It is" / "It was" → Subjective Case

✓
It was he who helped me. (not "him")
✓
It is I who am responsible. (not "me")

Rule 4: After "Than" → Depends on Meaning

✓
I know better than he. (subject comparison: I vs he)
✓
I know you better than him. (object comparison: you vs him)
✓

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (336 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: SSC tests narration in 4 ways: (1) statement, (2) interrogative, (3) imperative, (4) exclamatory.   • Statement: said → told, present → past, this → that
  • Question: said → asked, aux verb comes after 'if/whether' (yes-no) or wh-word
  • Imperative: said → ordered/requested/advised + to + V1
  • Exclamatory: said → exclaimed with joy/sorrow + that

Core Rule

When converting Direct Speech to Indirect Speech: (1) Remove quotation marks and use 'that' (statements) or 'if/whether' (yes-no questions); (2) Shift the tense of the reported speech one step back (Present → Past, Past → Past Perfect); (3) Change pronouns to match the new perspective; (4) Change time/place references (today → that day, here → there, now → then). Universal truths and habits keep their original tense.

Examples

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (375 PYQs)

Watch for: passive of interrogatives, imperatives, and sentences with two objects.

  • Simple Present: write/writes → is/are written
  • Simple Past: wrote → was/were written
  • Present Perfect: has/have written → has/have been written
  • Modal: can write → can be written; Imperative: Do it → Let it be done

Core Rule

In passive voice: (1) The object of the active sentence becomes the subject; (2) The main verb becomes 'be' (in the appropriate tense) + past participle (V3); (3) The original subject becomes the agent (by + object) — often omitted when unknown, obvious, or unimportant; (4) Only transitive verbs (verbs with objects) can be passivized. Examples

✓
Active: She writes a letter. → Passive: A letter is written by her.
Simple Present: writes → is written.
✓
Active: He has completed the project. → Passive: The project has been completed by him.
Present Perfect: has completed → has been completed.
✗
Active: Who broke the window? → By whom the window was broken?
→ Use 'By whom was the window broken?' — auxiliary 'was' must come before the subject in questions.
✓
Active: Open the door. → Passive: Let the door be opened.
Imperative passive uses 'Let + object + be + V3'.

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (202 PYQs)

  • Signal words: yesterday, last week, in 1999 → Simple Past
  • Signal words: since 2010, for 5 years, yet, already, just → Perfect
  • Signal words: by next year, by 2030 → Future Perfect
  • Sequence: If + Present, will + V1 | If + Past, would + V1 | If + Past Perfect, would have + V3
  • Stative verbs (know, believe, like, want, own) — usually not in continuous form

Core Rule

✗

✓
By next December, I will have completed my degree.
Future Perfect for action completed before a future point.
✓
She has been working since morning.
Present Perfect Continuous for action that started in past and is still continuing.

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (93 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tip: General S-V agreement rules beyond Rules 1-5, 11-13. Watch for: (1) collective nouns (committee, jury, family) — singular when acting as one unit, plural when acting individually; (2) uncountable nouns (news, furniture, information, advice, luggage) — always singular; (3) 'there' constructions — verb agrees with the following subject; (4) intervening phrases (with, along with, as well as) — verb agrees with the FIRST subject.

  • Uncountable (always singular): news, furniture, information, advice, luggage, baggage, equipment, machinery, scenery
  • Collective noun: The jury has decided (united) vs The jury have differed (individuals)
  • There-construction: There are five boys (verb agrees with 'boys', not 'there')
  • With/along with/together with/as well as: verb agrees with FIRST subject
  • Each/Every + noun + and + noun = singular: Each boy and girl is present

Core Rule

Subject-verb agreement requires the verb to match the subject in number and person. Special cases: (1) Uncountable nouns (news, furniture, information, advice) are always singular even when plural in form. (2) Collective nouns take singular verb when acting as one unit, plural when individuals are emphasized. (3) In 'there is/are' constructions, the verb agrees with the subject that follows. (4) When two subjects are joined by 'with, along with, together with, as well as, in addition to', the verb agrees with the FIRST subject. (5) 'The number of' is singular; 'a number of' is plural.

Examples

✓
The news is shocking.
'news' looks plural but is uncountable — singular verb.
✗
The committee were unanimous in their decision.
→ Use 'The committee was unanimous' — acting as one unit → singular.
✓
The captain, along with his players, is arriving.
'along with' — verb agrees with FIRST subject (captain, singular).
✓
There are many problems to solve.
'there' construction — verb agrees with 'problems' (plural).

✓

✗

Core Rule

The subjunctive mood expresses hypothetical, doubtful, or demanded situations. Three main uses: (1) Mandative subjunctive — after verbs of request, demand, suggestion, recommendation (suggest, insist, demand, recommend, require, propose), use the BASE form of the verb (no -s in third person singular): 'I suggest that he go' (not 'goes'); (2) Unreal past — after 'wish', 'if only', 'as if', 'as though', use past tense for present unreal situations, and 'were' for all persons: 'I wish I were rich'; (3) 'It is time' + subject + past tense: 'It is time we left' (meaning we should leave now).

Examples

✓
I suggest that he go now.
Mandative subjunctive — base form 'go' (not 'goes') after 'suggest that'.
✗
I wish I was rich.
→ 'I wish I were rich' — 'were' for all persons in unreal past (formal).
✓
He talks as if he knew everything.
'as if' + unreal → past tense 'knew' (he doesn't actually know).
✓
It is time we left.
'It is time' + past tense for present unreal.

Question Tags

Adding tag questions — auxiliary match, polarity reversal, special cases
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (8 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Question tags are short questions at the end of statements: 'You are coming, aren't you?' Rules: (1) positive statement → negative tag; negative statement → positive tag; (2) auxiliary matches main verb; (3) special cases: 'I am' → 'aren't I?'; imperative → 'will you?'; 'Let's' → 'shall we?'; (4) 'nobody/everyone/someone' → 'they' tag.

  • Positive → negative: You are coming, aren't you?
  • Negative → positive: You aren't tired, are you?
  • I am → aren't I: I am late, aren't I?
  • Imperative → will you: Close the door, will you?
  • Let's → shall we: Let's go, shall we?
  • Nobody/Someone → they: Nobody called, did they?
  • Everything/Nothing → it: Nothing happened, did it?

Core Rule

A question tag is a short question added to the end of a statement, used to confirm information or seek agreement. Formation rules: (1) Polarity reversal — positive statement takes negative tag ('You are coming, aren't you?'), negative statement takes positive tag ('You aren't tired, are you?'); (2) Auxiliary match — the tag uses the same auxiliary as the statement, or 'do/does/did' if no auxiliary; (3) Pronoun match — tag uses a pronoun matching the subject; (4) Special cases: 'I am' → 'aren't I?'; imperative → 'will you/won't you?'; 'Let's' → 'shall we?'; indefinite pronouns like 'nobody, someone, everybody' take 'they' tag; 'nothing, everything' take 'it' tag.

Examples

✓
You are coming, aren't you?
Positive statement → negative tag; auxiliary 'are' matches.
✗
I am late, amn't I?
→ 'I am late, aren't I?' — 'amn't' doesn't exist; use 'aren't I?'.
✓
Nobody called, did they?
'nobody' (indefinite pronoun for people) → 'they' tag.
✓
Let's go, shall we?
'Let's' → 'shall we?' tag.

Collocations

Words that frequently go together — make/do, take/bring, heavy/strong, etc.
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (19 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Collocations are words that naturally go together. SSC tests: (1) verb + noun (make a decision, take a risk, do homework); (2) adjective + noun (heavy rain, strong wind, high price); (3) verb + adverb (deeply regret, strongly recommend); (4) noun + noun (bus stop, water bottle). Wrong collocation = wrong answer. Memorize the common ones.

  • Make vs Do: make a decision/a mistake/a plan/a promise vs do homework/business/one's best/a favour
  • Take vs Bring: take (away) vs bring (toward)
  • Heavy: heavy rain, heavy traffic, heavy luggage, heavy heart
  • Strong: strong wind, strong coffee, strong opinion, strong feeling
  • High: high price, high speed, high temperature, high quality
  • Adverb collocations: deeply disappointed, highly recommended, widely known, fully aware

Core Rule

Collocations are words that frequently appear together in natural English; they 'co-locate'. There is no logical rule — collocations are conventions of usage. SSC tests four types: (1) Verb + noun (make a decision, do homework, take a risk, pay attention); (2) Adjective + noun (heavy rain — not 'strong rain', strong wind — not 'heavy wind', high price — not 'tall price'); (3) Verb + adverb (deeply regret, strongly recommend, highly appreciate); (4) Noun + noun (bus stop, water bottle, post office). Wrong collocation sounds unnatural even if grammatically correct. The best way to master collocations is through wide reading and memorization of common pairs.

Examples

✓
We had heavy rain yesterday.
'heavy' collocates with 'rain' (not 'strong', 'big', 'large').
✗
I want to make my homework.
→ 'I want to do my homework' — 'homework' collocates with 'do', not 'make'.
✓
She took a decision.
'take a decision' (British English) — also 'make a decision' is correct.
✓
He is deeply disappointed.
'deeply' collocates with emotions (disappointed, regret, concerned).

Redundancy & Wordiness

Avoiding redundant pairs and unnecessary repetition (e.g., 'return back', 'free gift')
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (15 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Redundancy = using words whose meaning is already included in another word. Common redundancies: 'return back' (return = go back), 'repeat again' (repeat = say again), 'free gift' (gift is free), 'end result' (result = end), 'advance planning' (planning is in advance), 'usual habit' (habit is usual), 'past history' (history is past), 'double comparison' (more better). Eliminate redundant words in sentence improvement.

  • return back → return (return already means 'go back')
  • repeat again → repeat (repeat already means 'say again')
  • free gift → gift (a gift is by definition free)
  • end result → result (a result is at the end)
  • advance planning → planning (planning is by definition in advance)
  • usual habit → habit (a habit is by definition usual)
  • past history → history (history is by definition past)
  • more better → better (double comparison)
  • most brightest → brightest (double superlative)
  • equally as good → as good (equally + as = redundant)

Core Rule

Redundancy is the use of words whose meanings are already included in other words in the same expression. Common SSC redundancies: 'return back' (return already means 'go back'), 'repeat again' (repeat already means 'say again'), 'free gift' (a gift is by definition free), 'end result' (result already means 'end outcome'), 'advance planning' (planning is by definition done in advance). Other forms of wordiness include: double comparatives/superlatives ('more better', 'most brightest'), double negatives ('I don't know nothing'), and tautological pairs ('each and every', 'first and foremost'). In sentence improvement, eliminate redundant words to make the sentence concise.

Examples

✓
Please return the book tomorrow.
'return' alone is correct; 'return back' is redundant.
✗
Please return back the book.
→ 'Please return the book' — 'return back' is redundant.
✓
She is the best student.
'best' alone is correct; 'most best' is double superlative.
✓
He repeated the question.
'repeated' alone is correct; 'repeated again' is redundant.

Relative Pronoun (General)

Who, whom, whose, which, that — choosing the correct relative pronoun
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (14 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Relative pronoun choice: 'who' (people, subject), 'whom' (people, object), 'whose' (possession, people/things), 'which' (things, with comma for non-restrictive), 'that' (people/things, restrictive only — no comma), 'where' (place), 'when' (time), 'why' (reason). Watch for: 'that' cannot introduce non-restrictive clauses (with commas); 'which' is preferred for things in non-restrictive clauses.

  • who: The man who called you is here. (subject)
  • whom: The man whom you called is here. (object)
  • whose: The girl whose book I borrowed is absent. (possession)
  • which: My car, which I bought last year, is red. (non-restrictive, things)
  • that: The book that I bought is red. (restrictive, things)
  • where/when/why: the place where...; the time when...; the reason why...

Core Rule

Relative pronouns introduce relative (adjective) clauses that modify a noun. Choice depends on: (1) Antecedent type — 'who/whom/whose' for people, 'which/that' for things, 'whose' for possession (people or things); (2) Grammatical role in the relative clause — 'who' for subject, 'whom' for object, 'whose' for possessive; (3) Restrictive vs non-restrictive — restrictive clauses (essential to meaning, no commas) can use 'who/which/that'; non-restrictive clauses (extra info, with commas) cannot use 'that' — must use 'who/which'. 'Whom' is formal; in informal English 'who' is often used. 'Where/when/why' introduce relative clauses of place/time/reason.

Examples

✓
The man who called you is here.
'who' = subject of 'called'.
✗
The man whom called you is here.
→ 'The man who called you' — 'who' is the subject, not 'whom'.
✓
My car, which I bought last year, is red.
Non-restrictive (comma) → 'which', not 'that'.
✓
The book that I bought is red.
Restrictive (no comma) → 'that' or 'which'.

Miscellaneous Grammar Patterns

Less frequent patterns: parallelism, compound adjectives, interjections, figures of speech, inversion variations, modifiers
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (24 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: This rule covers miscellaneous grammar patterns tested occasionally in SSC: parallelism (items in a list must be in the same grammatical form), compound adjectives (a five-year-old boy — hyphenated, singular), interjections (Wow! Oh!), figures of speech (simile, metaphor), inversion variations (Here comes the bus), modifiers (misplaced/dangling), 'lest' (negative purpose — lest we should fail), 'too' usage (too + adjective + to + V1).

  • Parallelism: She likes reading, writing, and painting (all -ing forms)
  • Compound adjective: a five-year-old boy (singular, hyphenated)
  • Lest: Walk carefully lest you should fall (lest + should + V1)
  • Too + adj + to: He is too tired to walk (so tired that he cannot walk)
  • Inversion (Here/There): Here comes the bus (verb before subject)
  • Misplaced modifier: 'He almost drove the car for six hours' vs 'He drove the car for almost six hours'

Core Rule

This miscellaneous rule covers less-frequent grammar patterns: (1) Parallelism — items joined by 'and/or' must be in the same grammatical form (She likes reading, writing, and painting — not 'to read, write, and painting'); (2) Compound adjectives — when a phrase acts as an adjective before a noun, hyphenate and use singular forms (a five-year-old boy, not 'five-years-olds boy'); (3) 'Lest' — negative purpose conjunction, followed by 'should + V1' or just V1 (Walk carefully lest you should fall); (4) 'Too' usage — 'too + adjective + to + V1' means 'so...that...cannot' (He is too tired to walk = He is so tired that he cannot walk); (5) Inversion with 'Here/There' — verb comes before subject (Here comes the bus); (6) Modifier placement — keep modifiers next to the word they modify to avoid ambiguity.

Examples

✓
She likes reading, writing, and painting.
Parallelism — all items in -ing form.
✗
She likes to read, writing, and paint.
→ 'She likes to read, write, and paint' — parallelism requires same form.
✓
He is a five-year-old boy.
Compound adjective — hyphenated, singular noun.
✓
Walk carefully lest you should fall.
'lest' + 'should' + V1 (negative purpose).
