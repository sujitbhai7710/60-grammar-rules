Singular Indefinite Subjects

Everybody / Everything / Nobody / Nothing / Somebody / Something / Anybody / Anything
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (41 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: SSC loves testing whether you recognize words like "everybody," "someone," "nothing" as singular. Watch for:

  • Verb agreement: Everybody is (not "are")
  • Pronoun agreement: Everyone should bring his (not "their")
  • With "but/except": verb agrees with the subject before the preposition
  • Nothing = things, Nobody = persons — don't mix them!

Core Rule

The subjects Everybody, Everything, Nobody, Nothing, Somebody, Something, Anybody, Anything are always singular. Hence, they always take a singular verb and singular pronouns.

Examples

✓
Everybody is ready to do his work.
Everybody is singular, so use "is" (not "are") and "his" (not "their").
✗
Anybody can do the work if they try
→ Replace with "he tries" because anybody is singular.
✓
Nothing but some books was available.
Here "but" acts as a preposition. The main subject before the preposition is "Nothing" which is singular.
✗
Nothing but some students was present
→ Replace "nothing" with "nobody" because some students are persons. Use "nobody" to indicate persons.
✓
Something is missing.
✓
Some things are missing.
Note: "Some things" is plural, unlike "Something" which is singular.
✓
Everybody who wants cake should pass his plate.
✗
Everyone section of the society is feeling the pinch.
→ Replace "Everyone" with "Every" because "section" is a noun and "everyone" is a pronoun. A noun and pronoun cannot come together — change pronoun to adjective "every".

Question Tag Rules

→
Somebody was present, weren't they?
Somebody/Nobody are used for persons — use plural question tag.
→
Something was present, wasn't it?
Something/Nothing are used for things — use singular question tag.
→
Nobody was present, were they?

Each / Every / Either / Neither / Any / None

Understanding quantity determiners
⭐⭐⭐☆☆ (31 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Each," "every," "either," "neither" are always singular. Common traps:

  • Each of the boys is (not "are")
  • Neither of the two is (not "are")
  • "None" can be singular OR plural depending on context
  • "A number of" = plural | "The number of" = singular

Key Distinctions

✓ When to Use
  • Each: 2 or more persons/things (pronoun & adjective)
  • Every: More than 2 only (always adjective)
  • Either: 2 persons/things (positive sentences)
  • Any: More than 2 (positive sentences)
  • Neither: 2 persons/things (negative sentences)
  • None: More than 2 (negative sentences)
✗ Common Mistakes
  • Every of the girls → use "Each" (need pronoun subject)
  • None of the two → use "Neither"
  • Any of the two → use "Either"
  • Neither of three → use "None"

Examples

✓
Each girl was present.
✓
Each of the girls was present.
✓
Every girl was present.
✗
Every of the girls was present.
→ Replace "every" with "each" — we need a subject (noun/pronoun) and "each" can act as pronoun, but "every" is only an adjective.
✓
Each student wishes to complete his work on time.
✓
Each of the students wishes to complete his work.
After "each of", use plural noun (students) but singular verb (wishes).
✗
None of the two students did the work.
→ Replace "None" with "Neither" — "none" is for more than 2, "neither" is for exactly 2.
✗
Mr. Sharma has three sons, neither of them is hardworking.
→ Replace "Neither" with "None" — three sons = more than 2.
✗
Any of the two students will complete the project.
→ Replace "Any" with "Either" — "either" is for 2 persons/things.
✓
None of the pens is working.
✓
Neither of them attended the function, did they?
✓
Please take one of each. (not "every")
✓
You gave me four toys, and none of them works. (negative sentence)

Both...And

Used in positive sentences only
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (7 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Both...and" always takes a plural verb:

  • Both Ravi and Sita are coming. ✓
  • Compare with "Either...or" → proximity rule applies

Core Rule

"Both" can be used as a pronoun and an adjective. With "Both," the verb is always plural. "Both" always pairs with "and" — never with "as well as."

Examples

✓
Both were present. (pronoun)
✓
Both the students were present. (adjective)
✗
Both the students as well as the teacher attended the meeting.
→ Replace "as well as" with "and" — "both" always pairs with "and".
✓
Neither of them went to the cinema. → No improvement (this is already correct)
✓
Both my brother and my friends love cricket.

Proximity Rule (Either-Or / Neither-Nor)

Verb agrees with the nearer subject
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (19 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: With "either...or" / "neither...nor," the verb agrees with the nearer subject:

  • Neither the teacher nor the students were (students = plural, nearer)
  • Neither the students nor the teacher was (teacher = singular, nearer)

Core Rule

When two subjects are joined by Either...or / Neither...nor / Not only...but also, the verb agrees with the subject nearer to the verb.

Examples

✗
Neither the colour or the size of the clothes suits me.
→ Replace "or" with "nor" — "neither" always pairs with "nor". By proximity rule, "the size" is nearer (singular), so verb "suits" is singular.
✓
Not only Ram but also his friends were present.
"friends" is nearer to the verb, so use plural "were".
✓
Either the students or the principal agrees to attend.
"principal" is nearer to the verb, so use singular "agrees".
✗
Neither of the scout leaders know how to trap wild animals.
→ Replace "know" with "knows" — "neither" takes a singular verb. Here "or" is correct because it connects phrase to phrase (conjunction).

📚 Extended Theory & Examples (from Nimisha Bansal's 120 Rules)

📖 From Nimisha Bansal Rule 35: If two subjects are connected by the words "or," "nor," "neither/nor," "either/o

If two subjects are connected by the words "or," "nor," "neither/nor," "either/or," or "not only/ but also", we use the verb according to the nearest subject.

Either the bears or the lion has escaped from the zoo. [Nearest subject "lion" is singular] Neither the lion nor the bears have escaped from the zoo. [Nearest subject "bears" is plural] Not only the teachers but also the Principal is happy. [Nearest subject "Principal" is singular] Not only dogs but also cats are available at the animal shelter. [Nearest subject "cats" is plural]

Numerical Words (Hundred / Thousand / Million / Dozen)

Adjective form stays singular; noun form can pluralize
⭐☆☆☆☆ (3 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Hundred," "thousand," "million" stay singular when used as adjectives:

  • Five hundred rupees (not "hundreds")
  • Two thousand people (not "thousands")
  • But: Hundreds of people (noun form = plural)

Core Rule

Words like hundred, thousand, million, dozen stay in singular form when preceded by a numeral (acting as adjective). They can be plural when used as a noun (with "of").

Key insight: When a word comes before a noun, it's an adjective — and adjectives don't have plural forms in English.

Examples

✓
She has donated five thousand rupees.
"Rupees" is noun. Words before nouns are adjectives. Adjectives don't pluralize.
✗
She has donated five thousands rupees.
→ Replace "thousands" with "thousand" — can't pluralize an adjective.
✓
She has donated thousands of rupees.
Here "thousands" is a noun (before "of"). Nouns can be pluralized.
✗
She brought two dozens eggs.
→ Replace "dozens" with "dozen" — "dozen" is acting as adjective here.
✓
She bought two dozen eggs.
✓
She bought dozens of eggs.
"Dozens" is a noun here (before "of").
✓
Dozens of eggs were bought.
✗
Two-thousands people were present.
→ Replace "thousands" with "thousand" — acting as adjective before noun.
✓
Thousands of people were present.
Correct — "thousands" is a noun before "of".

Until / Unless / If / Whether / That

Conjunctions for time, condition, doubt, and certainty
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (17 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: After "unless/until/if/whether," use Simple Present (not "will"):

  • Unless he works hard (not "will work")
  • If it rains, we will stay home (not "will rain")
  • Two future tenses NEVER in one conditional sentence

Key Distinctions

WordFunctionUsage
UntilConjunction & PrepositionFor time
UnlessAlways ConjunctionFor condition
IfConjunctionCondition (no "will") OR doubt ("will" OK)
WhetherConjunctionDoubt / Uncertainty
ThatConjunctionCertainty / Surety

The If + Not = Unless Rule

Important: If + not = Unless. Since "unless" already contains the negative meaning, never use "not" with "unless".

Examples

✓
I will wait until he comes. (conjunction — connects clause to clause)
✓
I will wait until Monday. (preposition — before a noun)
✓
If he comes, I will go. (condition — no "will" after "if")
✓
I don't know if he will come. (doubt — "will" is OK after "if")
✗
If they will come, I will go.
→ Remove "will" — here "if" expresses condition, not doubt.
✓
Unless he works, he will not succeed. (condition)
✗
Unless one are not punctual in one's work.
→ Replace "unless one are not" with "if one is" — "unless" is already negative, so "not" is redundant.
✓
I don't know if / whether he will come. (both correct)
✓
I don't know whether he will come or not. (use "whether" with "or")
"If" cannot be used with "or not".
✓
I have few doubts that he will come.
"few" = almost no doubt = certainty → use "that".
✓
I have a few doubts whether he will come.
"a few" = some doubt = uncertainty → use "whether".

No Double Comparatives & Superlatives

Never use two degrees of comparison together
⭐☆☆☆☆ (2 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Never combine two comparative/superlative forms — this is a very common SSC trap:

  • more better ✗ → better ✓
  • most best ✗ → best ✓
  • more easier ✗ → easier ✓
  • most easiest ✗ → easiest ✓

Examples

✗
He is more wiser than his brother.
→ Remove "more" — two comparative degrees ("more" + "wiser") cannot come together.
✗
Sachin is capable of doing more better work.
→ Remove "more" — "more" and "better" are both comparative.
✗
She is the most smartest girl in the class.
→ Remove "most" — two superlative degrees cannot come together.
✓
She is the most intelligent girl in the class. (correct — "most" + positive degree)

Special Note

More better ❌ but Much better ✔
Don't use "more" with "better," but "much" is acceptable with comparative forms.
✓
She is feeling much better.

"Comparatively" & "Relatively" Take Positive Degree

These words already imply comparison
⭐☆☆☆☆ (2 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Words like "comparatively," "relatively," "fairly," "rather" already imply comparison — use positive degree after them:

  • Comparatively good (not "better")
  • Relatively easy (not "easier")

Core Rule

The words "comparatively" and "relatively" already express comparison within themselves. Therefore, they take adjectives of POSITIVE degree (not comparative).

Examples

✓
The weather is comparatively hot. (not "hotter")
✓
The train is relatively slow. (not "slower")
✓
Earlier job opportunities were comparatively good. (not "better")
✓
It does more harm than good. (not "better" — positive degree before "than")

"As...As" Construction & Twice/Thrice

Twice/thrice/two times require "as...as" (not comparative)
⭐⭐☆☆☆ (12 PYQs)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: "Twice," "thrice," "two times" require "as...as" (not comparative):

  • He is twice as tall as me ✓
  • He is twice taller than me ✗
  • Structure: twice/thrice + as + adj + as

Core Rule

When sentences contain twice, thrice, two times, etc., use the "as...as" construction. When "as...as" is used for comparison, positive degree comes between them.

Examples

✗
He is twice taller than his brother.
→ Replace "taller than" with "as tall as".
✗
He is two times taller than his brother.
→ Replace "taller than" with "as tall as".
✓
He is two times as tall as his brother.
✗
He is equally as smart as his brother.
→ Remove "equally" — both "equally" and "as...as" express comparison; using both is redundant.
✗
How many eggs and how many money do you need?
→ Replace second "how many" with "how much" — "egg" is countable, "money" is uncountable. Use "many" with countable, "much" with uncountable.
✗
These all mangoes are ripe.
→ Replace "these all" with "all these" — "this/that/these/those" are demonstrative adjectives and come after "all".

  • past history → history (history is by definition past)
  • more better → better (double comparison)
  • most brightest → brightest (double superlative)
  • equally as good → as good (equally + as = redundant)

Core Rule

Redundancy is the use of words whose meanings are already included in other words in the same expression. ☆☆☆☆☆ (No PYQs yet)

🎯 Exam Tips & Common Traps

🎯 Exam Tip: Relative pronoun choice: 'who' (people, subject), 'whom' (people, object), 'whose' (possession, people/things), 'which' (things, with comma for non-restrictive), 'that' (people/things, restrictive only — no comma), 'where' (place), 'when' (time), 'why' (reason). Watch for: 'that' cannot introduce non-restrictive clauses (with commas); 'which' is preferred for things in non-restrictive clauses.

  • who: The man who called you is here. (subject)
  • whom: The man whom you called is here. (object)
  • whose: The girl whose book I borrowed is absent. (possession)
  • which: My car, which I bought last year, is red. (non-restrictive, things)
  • that: The book that I bought is red. (restrictive, things)
  • where/when/why: the place where...; the time when...; the reason why...

Core Rule

Relative pronouns introduce relative (adjective) clauses that modify a noun. Choice depends on: (1) Antecedent type — 'who/whom/whose' for people, 'which/that' for things, 'whose' for possession (people or things); (2) Grammatical role in the relative clause — 'who' for subject, 'whom' for object, 'whose' for possessive; (3) Restrictive vs non-restrictive — restrictive clauses (essential to meaning, no commas) can use 'who/which/that'; non-restrictive clauses (extra info, with commas) cannot use 'that' — must use 'who/which'. 'Whom' is formal; in informal English 'who' is often used. 'Where/when/why' introduce relative clauses of place/time/reason.

Examples

The man who called you is here.
'who' = subject of 'called'.
✗
The man whom called you is here.
→ 'The man who called you' — 'who' is the subject, not 'whom'.
✓
My car, which I bought last year, is red.
Non-restrictive (comma) → 'which', not 'that'.
✓
The book that I bought is red.
Restrictive (no comma) → 'that' or 'which'.
